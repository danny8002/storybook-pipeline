# The Legend of the White Snake

High among the cloud-wrapped peaks of Mount Emei, where ancient pines drank silver moonlight and mountain streams sang over mossy stone, lived a pearl-white serpent named Bai Suzhen. For a thousand years she meditated in quiet caverns, breathing morning dew and starlight to cultivate wisdom and mercy. When the millennium came to pass, a warm shimmer rippled along her scales, and she stepped forth in human form: a graceful young woman in snowy silk trimmed with pale jade, a white jade hairpin gleaming in her dark hair.

Yet immortality among lonely crags felt incomplete. Gazing down through the mist at lantern-lit towns, Bai Suzhen longed to understand human warmth, laughter, and love. Joined by Xiaoqing, a quick-witted green snake spirit who had become her sworn sister, she descended in early spring toward the famed city of Hangzhou.

Nowhere on earth was fairer than West Lake in April. Willow branches trailed into jade water, peach blossoms drifted on the breeze, and swallows skimmed beneath arched bridges. As Bai Suzhen and Xiaoqing strolled across the Broken Bridge toward distant Leifeng Pagoda, dark clouds suddenly swallowed the sun, and a silver curtain of rain swept across the lake.

Beneath a weeping willow stood Xu Xian, a gentle young apprentice from a local herbal shop, carrying a single oiled-paper umbrella. Seeing the two women caught unprotected in the rising downpour, he stepped forward with a shy, respectful bow.

"The wind off the lake turns cold when it rains, ladies," Xu Xian said, offering the bamboo handle to Bai Suzhen. "My shop lies nearby, and a little rain will do me no harm. Please take this to shelter yourselves."

As their fingers brushed against the handle, Bai Suzhen met his clear, earnest eyes. There was no pride or guile in him, only effortless kindness. Her thousand years of calm meditation stirred like lotus leaves in a warm breeze. Accepting the umbrella with a grateful smile, she asked where to return it, while Xu Xian hurried off through the mist, glancing shyly back.

Sunlight gilded the wet eaves next morning when Xu Xian called at Bai Suzhen's courtyard for his umbrella. Over fragrant Longjing tea, an hour slipped into an afternoon. Xu Xian spoke of his dream to heal poor villagers who could never afford grand physicians; Bai Suzhen listened with radiant warmth, moved by such compassion in a brief mortal life. Before the plum blossoms faded, they bowed beneath glowing red lanterns as husband and wife.

Together they moved to Zhenjiang and opened a modest herbal pharmacy called Baohetang. Warm light spilled across wooden herb drawers and brass scales as Xu Xian tended patients, while Bai Suzhen drew quietly on centuries of lore to blend remedies of uncanny power. When summer fever swept the riverbanks, Baohetang gave medicine freely to the poor. Grateful neighbors called her a living goddess of mercy, never guessing how close they came to the truth. With Xiaoqing's laughter in the courtyard and dried chrysanthemum scenting their rooms, Bai Suzhen believed peace would last forever.

High on Jinshan Mountain, however, stern eyes watched the town below. Fahai, abbot of Jinshan Temple, wore austere ochre robes and turned wooden prayer beads between his fingers. Steeped in rigid doctrine, he believed heaven had set an iron boundary between mortals and spirits; no matter how many lives Bai Suzhen saved, her presence among humans offended his sense of cosmic law.

One overcast afternoon, when Xu Xian climbed the temple steps to burn incense in gratitude, Fahai barred his path beneath the gate.

"Young benefactor, dark mist clings to your brow," the monk warned. "The woman sharing your roof is no mortal bride, but a thousand-year serpent spirit. Leave her at once and take refuge here, lest she cost you your life."

Xu Xian recoiled. "Venerable Master, you are mistaken! My wife tends the sick from dawn till midnight and harms not even an ant upon our doorstep."

Seeing devotion shield the young man's heart, Fahai narrowed his gaze. "Soon comes the Dragon Boat Festival, on the fifth day of the fifth moon, when spirits weaken at high noon. Persuade her to drink realgar wine, and let your own eyes judge the truth."

When the festival arrived, dragon boat drums thundered across the river and mugwort hung beside every door. Trusting her cultivation, Bai Suzhen stayed home to celebrate. At midday, Xu Xian set a cup of amber realgar wine before her with an anxious, hopeful smile.

"Just one cup, my dear, for health and long life together," he murmured, longing to prove the grim monk wrong forever.

Unable to refuse the man she loved, Bai Suzhen drank. Instantly, liquid fire tore through her veins, unraveling her magic like silk thread pulled loose. Whispering that she felt dizzy, she stumbled behind the pale ivory curtains of their bedchamber to rest.

Minutes later, carrying warm sobering soup, Xu Xian gently parted the curtains. The porcelain bowl shattered on the floorboards. Coiled upon the bed lay a great pearl-white serpent, scales gleaming faintly in the dim room. Terror seized Xu Xian's chest; with a strangled gasp, his heart failed from sheer shock, and he collapsed lifelessly beside the bed.

Waking in human form at dusk, Bai Suzhen found Xu Xian cold and breathless beside the spilled broth and cradled him with an anguished cry. Only one treasure could recall a departed soul: the celestial Lingzhi herb atop snow-bound Mount Kunlun.

Leaving Xiaoqing to guard her husband, Bai Suzhen flew through storm-lashed skies to Kunlun's frozen crags, where the sacred crimson-and-jade herb glowed upon a sheer cliff. As she plucked a stalk, the White Crane and Immortal Deer, guardians of the peak, swooped down with flashing blades. Weakened by the wine and carrying Xu Xian's unborn child, Bai Suzhen fought desperately through sleet and wind, shielding the herb with her own body as she sank to her knees in the snow.

Before the final blow fell, the Old Immortal of the South Pole descended on a radiant cloud and stayed his guardians' blades. Moved that a spirit would risk a millennium of cultivation for one mortal, the sage granted her the sacred herb. Returning before dawn, Bai Suzhen poured its warm golden essence past Xu Xian's lips. As color returned to his cheeks, Xiaoqing showed him a white silk sash he might have mistaken for a serpent in fever, easing his fear.

Yet Fahai remained relentless. Weeks later, he detained Xu Xian inside Jinshan Temple. Bai Suzhen and Xiaoqing rowed to the mountain's foot, pleading humbly for his release. Answered only with wrath, Bai Suzhen summoned surging river tides to flood the temple gates, yet childbirth pains weakened her against Fahai's golden barrier, forcing her retreat toward Hangzhou.

Having witnessed his wife brave heaven's fury for him, Xu Xian escaped with a young novice's help and found Bai Suzhen weeping on the Broken Bridge, where their story had begun. Kneeling to clasp her trembling hands, knowing her true nature at last, he swore never to leave her side again.

That autumn, Bai Suzhen gave birth to a healthy son, Xu Mengjiao. While soft lullabies still lingered in their home, blinding golden light filled the courtyard. Fahai stood at the threshold raising a sacred golden alms bowl humming with sealing power. Too weak from childbirth to resist, Bai Suzhen kissed her sleeping infant's brow and begged Xu Xian to raise their child in kindness. Drawn into the bowl's radiance, she was sealed inside a dark stone chamber beneath Leifeng Pagoda, condemned to remain until West Lake ran dry and the tower fell.

Twenty winters passed over the willows of Hangzhou. Gray touched Xu Xian's temples as he raised Mengjiao on stories of a mother whose compassion outshone the moon. Studying tirelessly beside an oil lamp night after night, the boy grew into an upright young man, journeyed to the capital, and won the empire's highest honor as *Zhuangyuan*, the supreme imperial scholar.

Wearing ceremonial scholar's robes and bearing imperial incense, Mengjiao came with his aging father to Leifeng Pagoda. Kneeling on the weathered stone steps, he bowed thrice to the earth, pouring twenty years of longing and filial devotion into his prayer. Moved at last by such unwavering love between spirit and mortal, heaven answered: warm golden light fractured Fahai's ancient seal, and the heavy stone chamber opened.

From the shadows stepped Bai Suzhen, timeless in ivory and jade, tears shining as husband and son rushed into her arms. Beside the shimmering waters of West Lake, beneath willows swaying in the sunset breeze, the family stood whole again, proving that love forged in mercy and sacrifice endures longer than stone.
